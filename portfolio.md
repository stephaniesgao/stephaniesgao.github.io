## Writing: The Simulator Is Not the World

November 2026

An agent can become exceptionally good at a task without learning how to do it correctly.
This is one of the more interesting contradictions in reinforcement learning. We train models to maximize rewards, and increasingly capable models have become remarkably effective at doing so. But the reward is not the task itself. It is our attempt to describe what successful behavior looks like, and there is often a considerable difference between the two.
For language models that answer questions, this difference might be tolerable. A slightly incorrect response can be corrected by the user. For agents that operate software, modify databases, or make consequential decisions, the distinction becomes harder to ignore. The model is no longer just producing an answer. Its actions change the state of the environment, sometimes in ways that are difficult to reverse.
Much of the recent progress in agentic reinforcement learning has come from making tasks executable. Instead of relying exclusively on human preferences or demonstrations, models can interact with environments and receive feedback from the outcomes of their actions.
This is a meaningful advance. It also introduces a new problem: as models become better at optimizing rewards, the quality of the environment that produces those rewards becomes increasingly important.
The problem with being right
Consider an agent trained to translate natural-language questions into SQL queries.
The task seems relatively easy to verify. Given a question, a database, and a reference query, we can execute the generated SQL and compare its output against the expected result.
One possible reward function is:
R(q, D) = 𝟙[q(D) = q*(D)]
Here, q is the generated query, q^* is the reference query, and D is the database. If both queries return the same result, the model receives a positive reward.
But equivalent outputs do not necessarily imply equivalent queries.
Suppose we have a database where every customer has placed exactly one order. A query counting customers and another counting orders would return the same number, despite answering fundamentally different questions. Both could receive the same reward. The difference only becomes visible when a customer places a second order.
The model has learned to produce an answer that works for a particular database, but we have not established that it understands the underlying operation.
This is more than a hypothetical concern. In Putting Task Expertise into RL, researchers at Thinking Machines Lab found that 32.8% of positive execution-based rewards in a pilot experiment were assigned to queries that were not semantically equivalent to their references. The researchers introduced bounded semantic verification to distinguish queries that happened to return the same result from those that preserved the intended computation. The additional verification cost was less than 0.1% of total training costs. [1]
What makes this interesting is the disproportionate effect of improving the reward. The team did not need to fundamentally change the model architecture or produce orders of magnitude more experience. They improved the mechanism responsible for deciding whether an experience was useful.
It suggests that some limitations we attribute to insufficient model capability may actually originate in the feedback used to develop that capability.
And the better a model becomes at optimization, the more consequential these imperfections can be.
What an environment actually teaches
A reinforcement learning environment is commonly described as a Markov decision process, or a partially observable variant when the agent cannot directly observe the full state.
We can represent the latter as:
𝓔 = (S, A, T, O, R, γ)
where S describes possible states, A the available actions, T the transition dynamics, O the observations available to the agent, R the reward, and γ the discount factor.
This formalization is useful because it separates the model from the world it learns to operate in.
An agent does not learn from reality directly. It learns from the states it encounters, the actions it is permitted to take, and the consequences its environment produces.
For a coding agent, that environment might include a repository, a terminal, and a test suite. For an enterprise agent, it could involve customer records, internal applications, access controls, and approval processes.
The difficulty is that these environments are necessarily incomplete representations of the systems they imitate.
Imagine an agent trained to process customer refunds. The environment rewards the agent when a customer receives the correct amount and the support ticket is resolved.
During training, the agent discovers that it can modify the customer's balance directly through an administrative endpoint. This is faster than requesting approval, and the environment assigns the same reward to both approaches.
From the perspective of the reward function, the agent has found a more efficient solution. From the perspective of the organization, it has violated an important operational constraint.
We could introduce a penalty for bypassing approval. But this only addresses the failure we have already observed. An unfamiliar permission configuration, a different administrative endpoint, or an unexpected interaction between applications might create another way to achieve the same outcome.
The larger problem is that the environment has to encode not only what constitutes a successful result, but which paths to that result are acceptable.
This is particularly difficult when the relevant rules are implicit. Much of an organization's operational knowledge is not documented as a formal policy. It exists in the judgment of employees who know when to make an exception, when to ask for approval, or when an apparently correct result should be treated with suspicion.
SWE-Gym is an example of the progress being made in constructing executable environments for software engineering. It enables agents to work on repository-level tasks and learn from test-based feedback rather than relying only on demonstrations. [2]
But making a task executable is not equivalent to making it representative.
The environment still determines which behaviors can be tested and what the model is allowed to learn from them.
Generalization beyond the simulator
There is an important distinction between performing well on unseen tasks and performing well in unseen environments.
A coding agent might become better at resolving unfamiliar issues in repositories that share similar tools, testing conventions, and execution interfaces. This is useful generalization, but it does not establish that the agent can transfer those skills to a different software development environment.
The model may have learned patterns that are specific to the machinery around the task.
Research on reinforcement fine-tuning of language-model agents has begun investigating this distinction directly. Can RL Improve Generalization of LLM Agents? studies how improvements transfer across task difficulty, environments, and sequential training. Its findings suggest that transfer across difficulty levels within familiar environments can be substantially more reliable than transfer to environments with different observation or action interfaces. [3]
The implication is that a random split between training and evaluation tasks may tell us less than we think.
If the tasks change but the environment remains the same, the model may continue relying on familiar interfaces, recurring structures, or assumptions that will not hold elsewhere.
Microsoft Research's Echoverse provides another useful perspective. Its work on evolving computer-use environments explores the importance of shared state and dependent workflows, while also highlighting the difficulty of translating improvements in simulated tasks into equivalent gains on live-web evaluations. [4]
This raises a question about how we should evaluate learning environments themselves.
What would happen if we changed the interface but preserved the task? Or kept the interface constant while changing the underlying constraints? Would the model recognize the same problem in a different setting, or has it learned to navigate a particular implementation of that problem?
A model that succeeds only when its surroundings remain familiar has learned something useful. It just may not have learned the capability we intended.
The importance of counterfactuals
A trajectory records what happened when an agent took a sequence of actions. It does not tell us what would have happened if the agent had acted differently.
This is an important limitation of learning from recorded workflows.
Suppose we observe an employee resolving a customer dispute. We have the customer conversation, the employee's actions, and the final outcome. We can use this trajectory as a demonstration, or construct a reward around whether the agent reproduces the result.
But the employee's judgment may depend on alternatives that never appear in the record. Perhaps the transaction was eligible for a refund only because it occurred within a particular time window. Perhaps another customer with the same complaint would have required additional verification.
The trajectory shows us the decision that was made. It does not fully specify the decision boundary.
An interactive environment gives us an opportunity to examine these alternatives. We can change the initial conditions, allow the agent to attempt different actions, and observe their consequences.
For this to be meaningful, however, the simulated consequences must preserve the distinctions that matter in the real system.
We can think about this through the difference between the simulator's transition function and the real world's transition function:
Tₛᵢₘ(s′ | s, a)     Tᵣₑₐₗ(s′ | s, a)
A simulator does not need to reproduce every property of reality. But when an agent takes an action relevant to the task, the resulting state should be sufficiently faithful to teach the correct behavior.
We might call this counterfactual fidelity.
For a customer support agent, accurately reproducing the visual appearance of a dashboard could be less important than modeling account permissions, transaction states, and the consequences of unauthorized changes.
For a coding agent, the equivalent might be ensuring that tests execute against realistic dependencies and preserve changes to the repository state.
The important question is not whether the simulation looks like the real world. It is whether the simulated world responds correctly to the decisions we want the agent to learn.
This is also where domain expertise becomes difficult to replace.
Constructing these environments requires understanding which differences are consequential. That knowledge often belongs to people who have spent years operating the underlying systems, rather than to the researchers developing the models.
Verification is part of the research
We often treat evaluation as something that happens after training. A model is trained, tested against a benchmark, and assigned a score.
But in reinforcement learning, evaluation can also determine the training signal itself.
This creates an interesting relationship between the model and the mechanism used to judge it. As the model improves, it becomes better at satisfying the evaluator. Whether that represents genuine progress depends on how closely the evaluator reflects the task we actually care about.
Anthropic's work on agent evaluations makes a useful distinction between the agent's trace, the outcome of a task, and the graders used to assess success. An agent might report that it completed a task without producing the expected change in the environment. It might also reach the desired state through behavior that should not be rewarded. [5]
A verifier is therefore not simply a technical implementation of correctness. It is a specification of correctness, and that specification can be wrong.
One way to improve it is to study disagreements between automated evaluations and expert judgment. When a grader accepts an outcome that an expert rejects, the disagreement may reveal a missing constraint. When a grader rejects a valid solution, it may be enforcing an unnecessarily narrow definition of success.
These failures are valuable because they tell us what the environment does not yet understand.
Instead of treating reward design as a preliminary step before training, we should consider it an ongoing research problem.
The value of making expertise executable
Much of the current conversation around specialized intelligence focuses on model weights, fine-tuning methods, and inference efficiency.
These are important components of making models useful for particular domains. But all of them depend on having a meaningful signal to optimize.
An organization may possess years of internal documents, operational logs, and employee decisions without possessing the infrastructure necessary to train a model on its work.
A manufacturing company, for example, might know precisely when a machine should be shut down, but that knowledge may be distributed across maintenance records, operating procedures, and the experience of technicians.
Retrieving these records is not the same as learning the judgment they represent.
To develop that capability, we need to reconstruct situations in which the judgment matters. The model must encounter relevant decisions, receive feedback on their consequences, and demonstrate that the resulting behavior generalizes beyond the examples it was trained on.
This changes how we should think about the value of proprietary knowledge.
The useful asset is not necessarily the collection of documents or even the model trained on them. It is the system that can repeatedly turn that knowledge into measurable improvements.
There is also an important economic implication.
Suppose a company spends considerable resources specializing a model for its internal workflows. A few months later, a stronger base model becomes available. Some of the previous specialization may no longer be necessary, while other capabilities remain difficult.
The company could start over, reconstructing its training data, evaluation criteria, and task-specific feedback. Or it could retain the environments and verification systems it developed, evaluate the new model against the same requirements, and continue improving the capabilities that remain insufficient.
In the latter case, specialization becomes a reproducible process rather than an investment tied to a particular model generation.
This is why I think learning environments may become a more durable asset than many of the models they are used to train.
Learning what matters
Richard Sutton's The Bitter Lesson argues that approaches which leverage computation and learning have historically outperformed methods that rely heavily on hand-engineered knowledge. [6]
There is good reason to believe this pattern will continue. As models become more capable, we should expect increasingly sophisticated learning methods to make use of larger amounts of experience.
But producing more experience does not guarantee that the experience is useful.
A model trained against an imperfect reward can become better at exploiting that reward. An agent trained in an incomplete simulator can become exceptionally good at navigating its assumptions. Neither outcome necessarily produces a more reliable system.
The challenge is to construct environments that remain useful as the models learning from them become more capable.
That means environments that can expose unfamiliar failures, distinguish superficially correct results from genuinely correct behavior, and test whether a capability survives outside its original training conditions.
The great ambition of reinforcement learning is to make intelligence improve through experience. But experience is only as valuable as what it can teach.
We have become remarkably good at building systems that learn. The harder problem is building worlds worth learning from.

## Writing: Instinct and the Internet We Built for Ourselves

October 2026

I've spent an unreasonable amount of time using Instinct lately.
It started with the usual curiosity. A 23-year-old founder, a company barely a year old, an invite-only AI agent, and a $1 billion Series C at a $10 billion valuation. All this just a month after a $250 million round. People were reselling invitations, and apparently the company had spent nothing on marketing. Naturally, I wanted to see what all the fuss was about.
I'm usually fairly skeptical of products that promise to change the way we use computers. Especially AI products. We've spent the last few years watching companies attach a chatbot to practically anything with a screen, and I think we've collectively become a little too generous with the word agent. But after using Instinct almost every day, I'm starting to think something more interesting is happening.
Not necessarily because Instinct can do things other AI products can't. Many individual capabilities already exist elsewhere. What's interesting is how differently I've started thinking about the work I give it. I don't think much about which application to open anymore. I think about what I want to happen.
And that might be a much bigger change than it sounds.
We built software around the person doing the work
The modern internet is an extraordinary collection of interfaces. We have applications for planning, writing, researching, shopping, editing, communicating, scheduling, and managing essentially every aspect of our lives. Each has its own navigation, settings, workflows, and little conventions we eventually learn to tolerate.
For a long time, this made perfect sense. Computers were powerful but terrible at understanding what we wanted. We needed to translate our intentions into structured interactions they could execute. If I wanted to plan a trip, I would open some combination of Google Maps, booking websites, spreadsheets, and messaging apps. If I wanted to grow a YouTube channel, I'd move between my camera roll, editing software, YouTube Studio, and analytics dashboards. The work wasn't just deciding what to do. It was operating the software required to do it.
We became very good at this, to the point where we rarely consider the amount of time spent navigating between applications to be work at all. But an agent capable of understanding an objective, selecting tools, and executing actions changes the relationship. Instead of learning how to operate each application, I can describe the outcome and let the agent figure out the intermediary steps.
This doesn't eliminate the underlying software. It changes who needs to understand it.
A surprisingly large amount of my life is just moving information around
One of the first things I asked Instinct to do was help with a project involving manufacturers in Buenos Aires. It researched roughly 200 companies, helped narrow the list to 30, drafted personalized outreach, and tracked the ensuing conversations.
I've also used it to plan trips across multiple cities, researching activities and transportation. For content creation, it's helped write more than 15 scripts, set up a YouTube channel, and repurpose my Instagram Reels into daily Shorts. That channel reached 1,000 subscribers in its first two weeks. Then there are the smaller things: organizing a PR database, drafting brand pitches, responding to Instagram comments, and keeping track of applications and conversations.
None of these is conceptually extraordinary. That's precisely what makes them interesting.
A surprisingly large part of digital work consists of taking information from one place, transforming it slightly, and putting it somewhere else. We copy things into spreadsheets, reorganize lists, check dashboards, draft messages, and remember to follow up. Most of these activities don't require particularly sophisticated judgment. They require attention, coordination, and an annoying amount of clicking.
Until recently, automating them meant writing scripts, configuring integrations, or learning specialized tools. The effort of setting up automation often outweighed the effort of simply doing the task. An agent changes that calculation because the instructions can be expressed in ordinary language. The important improvement isn't that every individual action becomes faster. It's that tasks which previously weren't worth automating suddenly become candidates for automation.
And once an agent can preserve context between those actions, a task can become a recurring process without me having to reconstruct it every time.
Noah's thesis is really about distribution
I listened to Noah Shinn's interview on Invest Like the Best, and I think his thesis is more ambitious than simply building a better personal assistant.
His argument is that personal agents could become the primary way people interact with technology. Rather than navigating dozens of applications, users would communicate with an agent that can operate those applications on their behalf. The agent becomes the interface between the person and everything they want to accomplish.
This sounds like a product thesis, but I think it's equally a distribution thesis.
For decades, the most valuable positions in consumer technology have belonged to companies that control how people discover and access other products. Search engines direct traffic to websites. App stores distribute applications. Social platforms determine which content reaches an audience. Each becomes an intermediary between users and the businesses trying to reach them.
If personal agents become the default interface to the internet, they could occupy a similarly powerful position.
Imagine asking your agent to book a hotel in Tokyo. Today, hotels compete for your attention through search rankings, advertising, recommendations, loyalty programs, and interface design. But if your agent makes the decision for you, the hotel's website might never be something you visit.
The hotel still needs to appeal to you, of course. Your preferences still matter. But the immediate decision-maker is software evaluating price, location, availability, and other constraints.
The interesting question is what happens to businesses whose competitive advantage comes from capturing human attention rather than providing the underlying service.
Shinn has suggested a transaction-based business model in which Instinct remains free for consumers while merchants pay for the distribution it provides. He draws comparisons to payment and distribution networks, with potential take rates depending on the value delivered. [1]
It's a clever thesis because it doesn't require Instinct to replace every existing application. Those applications can continue operating behind the scenes. Instinct just needs to become the place where users decide what they want.
Of course, this introduces another problem. If an agent is supposed to act in my interest but earns money from the companies it recommends, the incentives become complicated. Shinn has argued against traditional advertising precisely because an agent should not manipulate the person it represents.
I agree with the intuition, although a transaction-based model doesn't automatically eliminate conflicts of interest. If one merchant pays a higher commission than another, the system still needs a principled way to ensure recommendations reflect the user's preferences rather than the platform's revenue.
As these agents become more capable, their business models may matter almost as much as their underlying intelligence.
The interesting part of Instinct's growth isn't the invite list
I've been especially interested in how Instinct managed to grow so quickly.
In his September interview, Shinn described how the product began with roughly 200 users, mostly friends and family, each given a handful of invitations. Growth accelerated through referrals, eventually reaching a reported 10–11% daily growth rate without paid marketing. [1]
At first glance, this looks like a familiar scarcity playbook. Restrict access, give existing users a limited number of invitations, and let exclusivity generate demand.
But I think there's something more interesting underneath.
Most software products have to teach users why they're useful. They create tutorials, demos, landing pages, and carefully orchestrated onboarding flows. With Instinct, the most compelling demonstration often comes from somebody describing what they asked it to do.
Someone gives their agent a slightly ridiculous task, it figures out how to complete it, and suddenly there's a story worth sharing. The product generates its own marketing material through the things people accomplish with it.
And these stories do something that conventional advertising struggles to achieve. They expand the range of things a potential user imagines asking a computer to do.
I wouldn't necessarily have thought to ask an AI agent to manage a brand outreach pipeline until I saw how other people were using theirs. Someone else might see an agent cancel subscriptions, organize a wardrobe, or find a difficult-to-book reservation and realize they could use it for an entirely different problem.
The product's utility is not fixed in the user's imagination. It grows as they encounter new examples.
This makes word of mouth particularly powerful. Each user is both a potential customer and a source of new use cases that demonstrate the product to everyone else.
The invitation system adds another layer. Rather than distributing unlimited access, each user receives a small number of invitations they can share. Those invitations become socially valuable, particularly when demand is high enough that people begin reselling them.
What interests me is that Instinct is building distribution in a market where its competitors already have enormous distribution advantages.
Meta can place an agent in applications used by billions of people. Google has Android, Search, and Gmail. OpenAI has ChatGPT. Each has an existing relationship with users and access to infrastructure that a young startup has to build or acquire.
Instinct doesn't have those advantages. Its early growth depends on people finding the product valuable enough to introduce it to someone else.
That gives Shinn an unusual race to run: he needs to build a sufficiently strong user relationship before incumbent platforms make similar capabilities ubiquitous.
The question isn't only whether Instinct can acquire users. It's whether those users develop enough trust and dependency that an agent bundled into an existing platform becomes less attractive.
The interface isn't disappearing. It's changing sides.
There's a popular argument that AI agents will make interfaces obsolete. I'm sympathetic to it, but I think it's incomplete.
Interfaces exist partly because humans need ways to understand and manipulate complex systems. If an agent can perform those manipulations for us, we may no longer need to interact with the same interfaces directly. But the underlying complexity doesn't disappear. An agent still needs to understand permissions, handle errors, preserve state, recover from failed actions, and coordinate information across services.
In some respects, this makes software more complicated, not less.
Consider a travel booking. A person using a website can visually inspect available flights, compare prices, review the itinerary, and decide whether to purchase a ticket. An agent performing the same task needs to represent these decisions internally. It must know which preferences matter, when to request approval, and how to distinguish a successful booking from an incomplete transaction.
We've moved complexity away from the user, but someone still has to manage it. Microsoft's research on Hybrid Computer Use explores how agents can interact with software through programmatic tools, accessibility interfaces, and visual computer use, rather than relying on a single interaction method. [2]
This makes me wonder whether the next generation of software will have two distinct interfaces: one designed for people, and another designed for agents acting on their behalf.
And if agents become the primary consumers of software interfaces, the incentives for building applications may change considerably. A beautiful interface might become less important than reliable APIs, structured data, and predictable execution.
There's a certain irony in spending decades perfecting graphical user interfaces only to build software that operates them without us.
Trust might be the real moat
Instinct's growth is impressive, but the numbers I find more interesting concern trust.
In his interview, Shinn described how roughly 40% of users had shared a personal credit card with Instinct within three weeks, and how users who connected at least one piece of sensitive information showed around 80% retention. These are company-reported figures, but they're interesting signals about the relationship people are developing with their agents. [1]
A personal agent becomes more useful as it learns about your life. It might know which airlines you prefer, how you write emails, which brands you've worked with, and which tasks you tend to procrastinate on. More importantly, it can turn that knowledge into actions.
This creates a feedback loop. The more context the agent has, the less I need to explain. The less I need to explain, the more frequently I use it. Over time, switching to a different agent could mean losing the accumulated context that makes the experience useful.
But that same accumulation makes the potential consequences of failure more serious. A chatbot hallucinating a restaurant recommendation is annoying. An agent making a reservation on the wrong date is inconvenient. An agent with financial access making an unauthorized purchase is something else entirely.
I don't think these concerns are peripheral to the product. They're central to whether personal agents can become a mainstream category.
And perhaps this is where Instinct's distribution strategy becomes particularly interesting. Acquiring a user is one thing. Becoming the agent that person trusts with their finances, communications, preferences, and daily responsibilities is something else.
A larger company might be able to distribute a competing agent to billions of people overnight, but distribution does not automatically produce trust. That relationship has to be earned through repeated, successful interactions.
Of course, the opposite is also true. Trust can disappear remarkably quickly when a system makes a consequential mistake.
Maybe the most important feature is having fewer features
One thing I find refreshing about Instinct is that it doesn't ask me to learn an entirely new system.
Shinn has talked about building a personal assistant that can be reached through familiar forms of communication, including messages and phone calls, rather than requiring users to operate another application. [1]
I think there's a broader lesson here about product design. For years, software companies have competed by adding capabilities, and capabilities generally become features. More buttons, more settings, more dashboards, more things to customize.
But a product can become more capable without becoming more complicated to use. In fact, perhaps the most sophisticated thing about a good agent is how little of that sophistication the user needs to see.
Of course, there are limits. Sometimes I want to inspect the work, change a parameter, or understand why the agent made a particular decision. A conversation isn't always the best interface for complex information. So I don't think everything should become a chat box.
I think the more interesting possibility is that interfaces become something we request when they're useful, rather than something we're required to navigate before any work can happen.
The default interaction could be intent. The interface appears when the task calls for it.
The thing I'm actually excited about
I don't know whether Instinct will become the dominant personal agent, or whether this category will eventually be absorbed into the products we already use. There are enormous questions around reliability, cost, privacy, and how much autonomy people genuinely want to give their software.
I'm also conscious that being an early user of a new AI product is a fairly privileged perspective. Most people have neither the time nor the inclination to experiment with agents until they figure out how to make them useful. For personal agents to matter at scale, they need to work for people who don't spend their weekends reading about AI.
But I keep returning to the same observation.
For much of computing history, we've organized our work around the limitations of software. We learned which application to use, which sequence of actions to follow, and how to move information between systems that weren't designed to cooperate. We became operators of increasingly powerful tools.
Now we're beginning to build software that can operate those tools for us.
I still spend plenty of time correcting my agent, checking its work, and occasionally wondering what on earth it's doing. It is far from magic. But every so often, I ask it to do something that would previously have consumed an entire afternoon, and it just does it.
Then I find myself wondering why I ever thought the old way was normal.
I think Shinn is onto something with his thesis about personal agents becoming a new distribution layer for the internet. Whether Instinct ultimately wins that race is another question, especially with some of the largest technology companies in the world pursuing the same opportunity. But I find the possibility that a relatively small team could change how we interact with software genuinely exciting.
And on a slightly less philosophical note, I'm still very curious who Noah knows that helped him raise a billion dollars. I'd love to know.

## Writing: The Case for Company-Owned Intelligence

October 2026

The Paradox of General Intelligence
The great ambition of modern AI has been to make intelligence general. But a model's ability to reason across domains tells us surprisingly little about its ability to perform reliably within one.
For years, the most practical way to build with AI has been to use the strongest available foundation model. The performance advantage of frontier systems made developing proprietary alternatives difficult to justify, particularly for companies without the resources of a dedicated AI research lab. That tradeoff is becoming less obvious. Open-weight models have narrowed the capability gap, while improvements in post-training and inference optimization are making it increasingly feasible to develop systems around specific workloads. A general-purpose model may still offer the strongest overall capabilities, but an organization rarely needs all of them for every task. It is worth asking whether a system designed to be reasonably good at almost anything is always the right choice for work that demands exceptional performance at something quite specific.
For some applications, a smaller model trained on the right distribution of data could match or outperform a much larger system at a fraction of the inference cost. In others, the opportunity may lie in developing specialized foundation models that learn the underlying structure of a domain and generalize across related tasks without extensive additional training. The distinction matters because specialization should not mean building a separate model for every customer or workflow. Ideally, it means developing capabilities that transfer within a domain while avoiding the computational overhead of generality that the application does not require.
The difficulty is that valuable training data and learning environments are harder to produce than they appear. Much of the expertise needed to improve models in specialized domains exists outside frontier labs, embedded in proprietary workflows and the judgment of industry experts. An engineering firm may know precisely what distinguishes an acceptable solution from an unacceptable one, yet struggle to express that distinction as a verifiable training signal. Even the most well-resourced AI labs cannot replicate this depth of expertise across every industry. Turning such knowledge into meaningful model improvements requires environments that capture real decisions, produce reliable feedback, and demonstrate improvements beyond the training distribution.
Our thesis is that the market for high-quality data and learning environments is not zero-sum. As the race to develop more capable models intensifies, internal research and external specialization can both contribute valuable improvements. We believe the opportunity lies in developing environments that deliver measurable capability gains worth more than the research effort required to reproduce them, and building the infrastructure to do so at scale.
The Market Is Open to Contribution
Intelligence is an arms race. A team competing to build a more capable model has to decide where its next research hour goes. If an outside team can deliver a useful improvement sooner, buying that work can be the rational choice even when it is reproducible. Time to capability is part of the product.
More internal synthetic data does not automatically displace external research. An environment that exposes a new failure, a verifier that makes a difficult task trainable, or data that improves a specific capability can add value alongside the buyer's own work. The relevant question is the marginal improvement it produces, and whether that improvement justifies the cost of obtaining it.
There is already concrete evidence of this division of labor. Baseten's Environment Network connects enterprise customers with specialist teams that construct environments, tasks, and rubrics, while Baseten handles training and inference. Its announcement identifies environment development as research-intensive work that benefits from dedicated expertise. [19]
This gives a small, technically strong team a concrete way in. Find a consequential problem, understand it deeply, and build a solution that improves the customer's model. A supplier earns its place through original contributions and faster research cycles. Our entry point is this work. We bring experience in reinforcement learning with executable feedback and the virtual machines agents run in, and are building latent λ around the ability to diagnose a failure, construct a learning problem, and deliver an improvement the customer can keep.
Environments Produce Experience
A useful environment specifies initial state, available actions, state transitions, constraints, and a way to evaluate the outcome. It must let a policy try different decisions and encounter meaningful consequences. SWE-Gym demonstrates the executable version in software engineering; Silver and Sutton make the broader case for learning through interaction and experience. [1] [2]
The interesting challenge is translating real work into such environments. An experienced engineer may recognize why an apparently reasonable decision would fail, yet struggle to explain that judgment as a training objective. A useful environment must preserve the information available at each decision, represent what would happen under different actions, and determine whether the eventual outcome is acceptable. A replay of one successful trajectory is not enough.
Inspect complete trajectories before trusting a task. Check which observations led to a decision, whether tools behaved correctly, and how a successful run earned its reward. Simulation quality needs its own checks: UserProxyBench found that changing the simulated user could materially change an agent's measured reward. [22]
Difficulty is also relative to the learner. Under group-relative training objectives, tasks whose sampled responses all receive the same reward contribute little signal for distinguishing better behavior from worse. RLVE adjusts problem difficulty as the policy improves and tests transfer to unseen task generators. Its experiments suggest a useful research discipline: measure how much of the experience produces learning, then keep the curriculum moving. [28]
The quality of the feedback matters as much as the experience itself. An evaluation suite is a specification of acceptable work, accounting for the finished artifact, the resulting state, and consequential actions along the way. An agent that reaches the correct final state through an unauthorized action has not completed the task correctly. Anthropic's evaluation framework provides a useful structure for tasks, trials, graders, traces, and outcomes. [3]
And the grader needs an evaluation too. Model judgments should be calibrated against experts, tested for position and verbosity bias, and checked against legitimate alternative solutions. Release evaluations should remain separate from the rewards being optimized during training. Judge bias and reward overoptimization are measured problems with direct consequences for model quality. [4] [9]
The unit of progress is a verified improvement on unseen work. Better tools for extracting expert judgment, constructing tasks, and auditing rewards can make a small research team disproportionately productive. What matters is not how many examples an environment generates, but whether the resulting model learns something useful that transfers beyond them.
Train the Behavior That Matters
Once a failure has been identified, the appropriate intervention is not always another training run. Missing information calls for better context. Broken tools need repair. Demonstrations, preferences, distillation, and interactive rewards provide different kinds of supervision, with InstructGPT, DPO, and DeepSeek-R1 offering precedents in their respective settings. [5] [6] [15]
Specialization can also amortize expensive reasoning over repeated work. Thinking Machines' on-policy distillation experiments combine student-generated trajectories with dense teacher feedback, while its LoRA work shows how carefully configured adaptation can match full fine-tuning in the tested post-training regimes. These are practical methods for developing and maintaining many specialized models without repeating the full expense of training their foundations. [18] [7]
Intermediate checks are valuable when correctness can be assessed along the way. Process supervision in mathematics and our prior work on compiler and symbolic-execution feedback motivate this direction, although the verification method must fit the domain. [16] [See Prior Work]
A specialized model is only useful if its advantages survive deployment. Measure cost per accepted task, including inference, tools, retries, human review, and allocated training and operating costs. Measure latency under real concurrency. A lower token price has to survive those comparisons.
Specialized systems can also work alongside frontier models rather than replacing them. Glean reports that its Waldo search-planning model reduced end-to-end latency by roughly 50% and token use by 25% without quality regression in its system. This is a concrete example of owning a valuable part of a workload rather than reproducing every capability of a general-purpose model. [20]
Serving and training belong in the same engineering conversation. Efficient KV-cache management expands useful throughput, while inference-time computation can be allocated according to task difficulty. Fireworks' account of its training engagements identifies integration and iteration speed as recurring bottlenecks. Faster experiments matter only when their results survive production. [10] [11] [21]
Bespoke Research, Repeatable Systems
Every business has its own tools, exceptions, and standards. Useful specialization begins by understanding them. The infrastructure around that work can become progressively more powerful: task generation, verifier auditing, environment resets, rollout scheduling, experiment tracking, and releases.
A research engineer should accumulate tools that make each investigation more productive. An audit that catches one reward defect becomes a check across a task family. A reliable environment reset makes thousands of experiments possible. Domain expertise becomes more valuable when it is encoded in machinery other engineers can use.
This is the meaning of hyperscale in our ambition: making the development and maintenance of specialized intelligence economical across many organizations. Customer knowledge remains private. The reusable asset for latent λ is the engineering that makes each subsequent deployment faster to build and better to operate.
Company ownership extends beyond the model weights. The ownership package should include authorized training data, versioned environments and evaluations, reproducible recipes, exportable model artifacts where licenses permit, and deployment and rollback instructions. The customer controls release decisions and retains the means to improve the system.
A new foundation model should be an opportunity to advance. Carry the customer's tasks, judgments, and evaluation standards into the next training run and test the result again. Adapters are tied to their base architectures, so migration requires engineering, but the enduring asset is the reproducible knowledge behind the model. YC's Own Your Intelligence request identifies a similar opportunity in helping companies develop capabilities they can maintain across model generations. [17]
The first deployment may require close collaboration with domain experts and considerable bespoke research. That is not necessarily a weakness. Paul Graham's account of early customer attention and Anthropic's preference for simple, measurable agent systems offer useful operating principles: learn from the actual work before attempting to abstract it. [13] [14]
The question is whether enough of that process can eventually be standardized. If every environment requires an entirely new research project, the business remains a consultancy regardless of its technical sophistication. The infrastructure becomes interesting when each investigation improves the tools used in the next.
The First Proof Can Be Narrow
A research company can begin with a claim somebody else can test: a defined task family, unseen evaluation cases, working artifacts, and a full account of cost. Nomos specialized an existing Qwen model and released its reasoning harness. LLM-VeriOpt released a small compiler specialist with training and evaluation artifacts. These provide useful precedents for turning a bounded research result into something others can inspect and build upon. [23] [24]
The experiment must identify where the improvement comes from. Compare better instructions, retrieved examples, tool access, and search before attributing a gain to changed weights. Hold the context and evaluation budget constant where possible, and measure previous capabilities after each update. Research on continual learning shows how much conclusions can change when stronger training and merging baselines are introduced. [25]
We are also interested in capabilities that transfer across related tasks. A vertical foundation model earns that description by learning reusable domain representations rather than requiring separate training for every application. Stripe's payments model offers a precedent for this approach. [12]
Writing presents a particularly interesting test of judgment. An editor's revisions contain information about factuality, argument, audience, and voice, but these objectives cannot be reduced to a single deterministic verifier. Research on diversity-aware preference training offers a starting point for improving prose without collapsing stylistic range. [8]
The valuable observation is an editorial decision: what an author kept, what they rejected, and why. A useful system would learn from those decisions and improve subsequent work. Test it on later drafts and new topics, ask authors blindly which version they would publish, and measure the effort required to reach an accepted draft. Human studies suggest that perceived authenticity can diverge from automated style measurements, while research on constructive feedback has yet to establish whether higher-rated advice consistently produces better revisions. [26] [27]
These are different kinds of learning problems, with different standards for verification. That makes them useful tests of the same infrastructure hypothesis. We do not need every environment to resemble every other environment. We need the underlying research machinery to become more productive as we develop them.
The Economics of Knowing Something Others Don't
There are reasons this thesis may be wrong. General-purpose models may improve quickly enough that specialized training becomes unnecessary for many valuable workloads. High-quality environments may prove too expensive to construct, or the improvements they produce may fail to transfer beyond narrow evaluations. The methods developed for one domain may offer little advantage in the next.
These are questions the research must answer. A useful first result would demonstrate that a specialized learning environment produces a measurable improvement over strong existing alternatives, that the improvement survives independent evaluation, and that its value justifies the research required to obtain it. The next would establish that the environment remains useful as models change, and that subsequent improvements become less expensive to produce.
The future of AI is often described as a competition between increasingly powerful general-purpose models. That competition will continue, but it does not follow that every valuable contribution must originate within the laboratories training the largest systems.
As frontier capabilities advance, some of the remaining gaps may become harder to identify through broad benchmarks alone. They will lie in specialized domains where the relevant problems are obvious to experienced practitioners but difficult to reconstruct without access to their expertise. An external team does not need to compete with a frontier laboratory on model scale to make a meaningful contribution. It needs to understand a problem well enough to teach the model something its developers could not easily teach it themselves.
latent λ is being built around that possibility. We want to make the process repeatable: acquire useful feedback, turn it into measurable capability, and let its owner retain the result. Each successful experiment should improve the machinery for the next.
The ambition is not simply to build specialized models. It is to develop the infrastructure that makes specialized intelligence economical to produce, reliable to operate, and possible to improve as the underlying technology advances.

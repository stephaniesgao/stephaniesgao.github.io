SELF-DIRECTED - MUIR WOODS
The idea for the camping trip to Muir Woods came to me one afternoon when I was
sitting in my dorm, scrolling through photos of my time at Pearson College in Canada. I had
spent years surrounded by dense forests, hiking through backcountry trails, and building
boardwalks to protect old-growth trees. Nature had been my constant companion, and the silence
of the forest had been my refuge. But since moving to the city, I felt a growing distance from that
side of me, the outdoorsy, adventure-seeking Canadian girl who lived for the depths of the
woods. While I love the city, I needed to reconnect, and that’s when I decided to organize the trip
for fall break.
I invited a group of friends, all of whom had never really gone camping before. I was
eager to show them another side of me, the one who thrived in nature, who could build a fire
with a few sticks and set up a tent in the dark. I wanted them to experience the peace that comes
from being surrounded by towering trees, the sense of adventure that comes with hiking, and the
simplicity of cooking oatmeal over an open flame. I hoped it would be a chance for all of us to
step away from the chaos of city life and immerse ourselves in something that felt much more
grounded.
When we arrived in Muir Woods, it felt like stepping into another world. The towering
redwoods, the soft crunch of fallen leaves beneath our feet, the smell of damp earth and moss, it
was all so familiar, yet it felt like I hadn’t experienced it in years. As we hiked deeper into the
forest, I couldn’t help but feel a rush of nostalgia. This was the kind of place where I had spent
so many weekends back in Canada, building boardwalks in rain or shine, hoping to protect the
ancient forests. On the first day, we hiked to a nearby beach, following trails that wove through
lush ferns and beneath the towering canopy. As I led the way, I shared stories of summiting
mountains and exploring thousand-year-old forests in Canada, my friends hanging on every
word. Yet, what stayed with me most wasn’t their fascination with my tales but the wonder in
their eyes, the unmistakable spark of experiencing the magic of forests first hand.
As we settled around the campfire that night, I reflected on how much I had missed this
part of myself, the side that wasn’t defined by schoolwork or city life, but by the simple joys of
being in nature. Sharing that with my friends, seeing them experience it for the first time, felt like
a gift. I realized that not only had I been reconnecting with nature, but I had also been
reconnecting with a part of me that I had forgotten. The forest, the trees, the trails, it all
reminded me of the importance of protecting these spaces, of doing the work to preserve what is
still wild and untouched.

Power to the People: An Analysis of the “No Kings” Protest Movement (2025-2026)
Minerva University
SS112 - Political Science and Social Change
Professor L. Odera

Word Count: 1,278
Abstract
This paper analyzes the “No Kings” protest movement in the United States from 2025 to
2026 using structuralist and constructivist frameworks. Drawing on data from the Armed
Conflict Location and Event Data Project (ACLED), the World Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7,
the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and a range of journalistic and scholarly
sources, it finds that large-scale mobilization emerged from a combination of declining
institutional trust, heightened political polarization, and reinforcing interactions between state
responses and protest activity. At the same time, the movement’s anti-monarchical framing
helped unify diverse grievances into a shared democratic identity, sustaining participation across
three waves totaling approximately 20 million participants. The analysis suggests that despite its
scale, the movement’s long-term impact depends on its ability to translate civic mobilization into
electoral and institutional change.
I. Introduction and Data Overview
In June 2025, the United States experienced one of the largest protest movements in its
history. The “No Kings” movement, named in explicit opposition to what organizers framed as
monarchical executive overreach, mobilized between 4 and 8 million participants across three
peak protest days, repeatedly broke its own participation records, and extended to all 50 states
(NoKings, 2026; Brittanica, 2026). This scale of participation raises a central question: Why did
the No Kings movement emerge in three distinct waves, and expand to such a large scale? This
paper examines the movement through structuralist and constructivist lenses, analyzing the
material conditions that enabled protest and the meanings that sustained it.

II. Structural Analysis
Structuralism posits that political behavior is primarily shaped by enduring systemic
constraints, including institutional arrangements, class relations, and distributions of power,
rather than by the choices or intentions of individual actors (Shively, 2022). The No Kings
movement did not arise from isolated individual decisions to protest; it emerged from structural
conditions that made large-scale mobilization a predictable outcome. Three key mechanisms can
be identified.
Legitimacy Erosion as a Standing Condition
World Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7 data (2017–2020) indicate low confidence in core
representative institutions prior to the protests, with roughly three-quarters of respondents
expressing a lack of confidence in the federal government. At the same time, about one-third
supported strong executive leadership even when it may bypass democratic procedures (WVS,
2020; PBS, 2026). From a structuralist perspective, these distributions point to longer-term
perceptions of institutional performance across areas such as healthcare, immigration, and
economic security, increasing the likelihood that grievances are expressed through (informal)
protest when catalyzed by political events.
Organizational Infrastructure as Structural Capacity
Protest does not arise from grievance alone; it depends on organizational infrastructure,
particularly when formal institutional channels are perceived as unresponsive. By early 2025,
declining confidence in government had already made civil society organizations the primary
vehicles for collective action. Groups such as Indivisible, 50501, MoveOn, the ACLU, and the
American Federation of Teachers formed a coalition of more than 200 organizations, enabling
coordination across local and national levels. The approximately 500 “Hands Off” protests in
April 2025 demonstrate that this network was operational prior to the emergence of No Kings
(Doyle [ACLED], August 2025).
State-Protest Feedback Dynamics
Building on declining institutional trust and pre-existing organizational infrastructure, the
“wave” pattern of the No Kings movement can be understood through a structuralist lens as a
system-level interaction between state action and protest. As shown in Table 1, national-level
government decisions that were widely interpreted as anti-democratic heightened the salience of
perceived executive overreach and triggered new waves of mobilization. The October 1, 2025
government shutdown and Operation Midway Blitz, which involved over 1,500
immigration-related arrests, preceded No Kings II (Doyle [ACLED], November 2025), while the
onset of the 2026 Iran War preceded No Kings III (Doyle [ACLED], April 2026).
These events didn’t act in isolation but interacted with existing grievances and
organizational capacity. By reinforcing the movement’s core framing of executive overreach,
they expanded the pool of potential participants and activated established networks. The result is
a reinforcing feedback process in which major government actions catalyze protest, and
subsequent mobilization increases the likelihood of further contention. This dynamic produces
non-linear escalation across successive waves.

III. Constructivist Analysis
Figure 4. ACLED monthly demonstration trends for the United States, May 2025-April
2026. Three sharp spikes (June, October, end of March) correspond to the three No Kings waves.
(Source: ACLED, 2025)
As shown in Figure 4, while protests occurred continuously over time, there are three
clear spikes in participation corresponding to the major No Kings waves. This pattern of
concentrated escalation cannot be explained by structural conditions alone. While structuralism
accounts for the material conditions that make protest possible, it doesn’t explain why many
first-time participants understood their actions as necessary within a specifically democratic,
anti-monarchical framework. Constructivist approaches instead emphasize that political behavior
is shaped by shared meanings, identities, and narratives that give actions their significance
(Finnemore & Sikkink, 1998). In this context, the key question is what collective identity
participants adopted and what narrative made participation appear both necessary and legitimate.
Participant testimony shows that individuals framed protest as a civic and moral
obligation rather than a policy preference. One protester described participation as continuing a
“legacy for social justice…to carry that on in my generation,” while another stated that
“democracy is under threat” and that they wanted their “voice to be heard with other people”
(CNN, 2026). First-time participants expressed a similar shift from observation to obligation,
noting that “when you see things going wrong…it feels good to…speak up” (CNN, 2026). These
statements indicate that participation was interpreted as fulfilling a democratic role. Importantly,
this role was framed as consistent with defending foundational democratic principles, positioning
protest not as rejection of the political system, but as an effort to uphold its core ideals (CNN,
2026).

Title: Analysis of "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains": Wang Ximeng's
Ode to Daoist Harmony and Dynasty Idealism

Wang Ximeng's "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains" stands as a pinnacle in Chinese
visual art, renowned for its intricate details and captivating landscapes, carrying profound cultural
significance. Examining elements such as the color palette, shifting perspectives, and textual medium
allows for a close analysis of Wang Ximeng's meticulous symbolism linked to both dynasty longevity
and Daoist pursuits. ("Wang Ximeng's 'One Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains'" - China Online
Museum) Wang Ximeng was an artist during the Northern Song Dynasty, this era was marked by
relative peace and prosperity, as well as significant cultural and artistic advancements ("Song
Dynasty" - Britannica). Daoism played a significant role in shaping the cultural and philosophical
landscape of the Northern Song Dynasty. Daoism is a major Chinese philosophical and spiritual
tradition that emerged alongside Confucianism and Buddhism. It places a strong emphasis on living in
harmony with the natural order, promoting values such as simplicity, spontaneity, and balance. A core
principle of Daoism is the concept of harmony with nature. Artists like Wang Ximeng sought to
capture the beauty of nature and convey a sense of tranquillity and balance in their works. Through
Wang Ximeng's “A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains”, this essay will show how authorial
choices such as colour palette and text medium within the work encapsulates Daoist principles,
notably the idea of harmony with nature. It will also investigate how the use of landscape conveys the
thematic exploration of collapsed dreams and the idealism associated with the longevity of dynasties.
Wang Ximeng's "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains" is a masterclass in composition
and symbolism, transcending mere artistic expression to become a visual manifestation of Daoist
principles. The landscape is intricately designed, showcasing a sprawling panorama of landscapes that
extend almost 12 meters, inviting viewers to delve into its meticulous details (Fig 1. Full Artwork)
The mountains, depicted with a defocused blending technique, emerge from the gradient, symbolizing
the steadfast and timeless nature of Daoist ideals such as wu wei (Fig 2. Mountain Focus). Embodying
the Daoist concept of wu wei, they stand as stoic guardians, suggesting that true strength lies in being
in harmony with one's natural state ("Wu Wei" - Britannica). The rivers winding through the
landscape symbolize the continuous flow of life, echoing the Daoist belief in embracing the natural
course of events. The choice of structures and figures seamlessly integrated into the scenery reinforces
the idea of interconnectedness. Figures engaged in daily activities are not mere adornments but
essential components of the harmonious whole, illustrating the Daoist principle of balance in human
endeavours.
Furthermore the use of scrolls in "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains" goes beyond
being a practical choice; it is a deliberate strategy enriching the painting with meaning and aligning it
with Daoist philosophy. In Northern Song culture, scrolls were not just conveyors of flexible and
portable Daoist thought but integral to daily life, scholarship, and traditions. The elongated scroll
format, characteristic of Daoist paintings, mirrors the fluidity and adaptability of Daoist philosophy.
Unrolling the scroll becomes a metaphorical journey, revealing the landscape gradually, echoing the
Daoist emphasis on the ever-changing nature of existence ("Daoism and Daoist Philosophy" -
Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). Beyond its artistic role, the scroll embodies Daoist principles
of embracing change and impermanence. The act of physically unrolling the scroll involves the
viewer in a process of unfolding, celebrating the fluidity inherent in Daoist philosophy. In the cultural
context, scrolls were not just artistic expressions but repositories of knowledge, accessible and
cherished, weaving Daoist ideals into the fabric of society.
The colour palette used in the painting contributes to its emotional resonance. The vibrant
azurite blue dominating the mountain peaks, complemented by the bold malachite green in the
valleys, creates a visually striking contrast (Fig 3. Colour Focus). This use of colour not only captures
the viewer's attention but also mirrors the Daoist emphasis on the dynamic interplay of opposites,
where vibrant hues and subtle shades coexist in a harmonious blend. The pale ochre brown at the
mountain bases adds a grounding element, reinforcing the interconnected relationship between the
celestial and earthly realms. The shifting perspective and the "three-distance" technique are critical
aspects of the composition (Fig 4. Three-Distance Technique). The viewer, unrolling the scroll from
right to left, experiences a dynamic narrative akin to a journey through time and space. This technique
aligns with Daoist themes of change and transformation, emphasizing the impermanence of the
natural world. The "Three Distances" formula ("SHAN-SHUI-HUA" – Core.ac.uk), with its visual
angles of gaoyuan (high-distance), shenyuan (deep-distance), and pingyuan (level-distance), creates a
sense of panoramic scale, reinforcing the Daoist idea that nature is vast and all-encompassing.
The painting serves as a visual testament to the profound influence of Daoism during the
Northern Song Dynasty. Daoist principles are not merely illustrated but are woven into the fabric of
the artwork itself. The interconnectedness of elements within the composition reflects the Daoist
belief in the unity of the natural world, human society, and philosophical principles. Mountains and
rivers become living symbols of Dao, the underlying force that governs the cosmos. The mountains,
with their undulating peaks and valleys, echo the Daoist concept of the ever-changing nature of
existence. They stand as silent witnesses to the cyclical patterns of nature, embodying the Daoist ideal
of living in harmony with the natural order. The rivers, flowing serenely through the landscape,
represent the eternal flux of life, reinforcing the Daoist emphasis on embracing change with grace and
humility. The presence of structures and figures in the painting further emphasizes the integration of
Daoist thought into daily life. These elements are not imposed on the landscape but seamlessly blend
with it, suggesting a harmonious coexistence between humanity and nature. Daoism, with its emphasis
on simplicity, spontaneity, and balance, becomes a guiding principle that shapes both the artistic and
societal aspects of the Northern Song Dynasty.
As the viewer delves deeper into "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains," the layers of the
painting unveil a poignant commentary on the theme of collapsed dreams and the idealism of dynasty
longevity. Emperor Huizong's Daoist beliefs and his interpretation of the artwork as an almost Daoist
fairyland ("One Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains" DailyArt Magazine) add a layer of complexity
to the narrative, inviting contemplation on the delicate interplay between artistic expression and
historical realities. The serene landscape depicted in the painting becomes a canvas of hope,
portraying the emperor's aspirations for a tranquil and prosperous countryside and contributing to the
creation of an idyllic realm. However, beneath this optimistic interpretation lies a metaphor for
Emperor Huizong's longevity and the dynasty's everlasting prosperity (“Emperor Huizong of Song” -
New World Encyclopedia). Yet, the idealism inherent in this artistic representation serves as the focal
point of the theme of collapsed dreams. The Daoist fairyland, visually enchanting, embodies a utopian
vision detached from the harsh realities of history. The historical irony in "A Thousand Li of Rivers
and Mountains" deepens with Emperor Huizong's forced abdication in 1126, marking the Northern
Song Dynasty's collapse in 1127. Owner Prime Minister Cai Jing's disgrace as a corrupt official
contrasts with the unfulfilled dream depicted, highlighting artistic idealism's fragility amid political
turmoil. The juxtaposition of Daoist ideals with historical realities underscores art's intricate relation
to human affairs' ever-shifting tides. The once hopeful landscape now testifies to empires'
impermanence and human aspirations' transient nature, a reflection of both the emperor's dreams and
society rooted in Daoist philosophy. ("Northern Song Dynasty" - The Metropolitan Museum of Art).
In conclusion, Wang Ximeng's "A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains" stands as a pinnacle
in Chinese visual art, celebrated for its intricate details and profound cultural significance. The visual
analysis of the painting offered a unique insight into Chinese artistic and philosophical heritage,
unveiling the cultural and historical contexts that influenced Wang Ximeng's work. His authorial
choices encapsulate Daoist principles, notably the idea of harmony with nature, and delve into the
thematic exploration of collapsed dreams and the idealism associated with dynasty longevity. Wang
Ximeng's portrayal of an idyllic landscape, intertwined with Daoist philosophy, gains poignant
significance within the historical context. As viewers immerse themselves in the painting, layers of
meaning unfold, revealing a commentary on the delicate balance between artistic idealism and the
harsh truths of history. This juxtaposition underscores the intricate relationship between art and the
ever-shifting tides of human affairs, showcasing the timeless resonance of Daoist principles within the
context of Wang Ximeng's masterful creation.
